# Evidence: Interviews and Interview Preparation

Use in **drafts** only (cite as `[^n]`, definition line copied from the Bibliography). Never cite in the final prep sheet.

## Rules
- Sources tagged `[snippet]` were read from search results only.
- The research below is about how **employers** select people. I found no controlled study showing how much *candidate* preparation improves outcomes. Say "commonly advised", not "proven".
- Never promise an outcome. Evidence supports a preparation method, not a result.

## Findings

### A. Why structured, story-based preparation is sensible
1. Sackett et al. (2022) re-analysed decades of selection research and found structured interviews to be the top-ranked single selection procedure for predicting job performance [1][2][snippet]. — High (peer-reviewed). **Implication: many employers now use the same questions and scoring rubrics for every candidate. Prepare specific, evidence-rich answers rather than rehearsed generalities.**
2. Earlier meta-analysis found structured interviews more valid than unstructured ones [3][snippet]. — High (peer-reviewed, 1994)
3. A secondary summary says low-structure interviews are only slightly better than chance, and that a well-structured interview out-predicts other single methods. I did not verify these figures against the paper [4][snippet]. — Low. Use finding 1 only.

### B. STAR / SAR answers (commonly taught)
4. STAR = Situation, Task, Action, Result. Career services present it as the standard way to answer behavioural questions [5][6][7]. — Medium (consistent professional guidance)
5. Spend most of the answer on the action, and slightly less on the result; keep situation and task brief [7][8]. — Medium (advice)
6. Use "I" statements. Heavy use of "we" makes it hard for an interviewer to see your own contribution [9]. — Medium (MIT career services)
7. A commonly advised length is about two minutes per story [10][snippet]. — Low-Medium (coaching source)
8. UC Davis advises brainstorming stories from work, projects, teams, activities and leadership, and preparing examples about problems, failures and difficult teams or supervisors [11]. — Medium
9. Career services describe types of questions: traditional/general, behavioural, situational and role-specific problem-solving [11][12]. — Medium

### C. Logistics and follow-up
10. Waiting about five business days before a follow-up email is the commonly advised default, unless the employer gave a timeline [13][14]. — Low-Medium (career-advice site)
11. A thank-you note within 24-48 hours of the interview is commonly advised [15][snippet]. — Low-Medium
12. Prepare questions to ask the employer, such as about success measures, team, culture and process [16][snippet]. — Medium

### D. Ethics
13. Treat interviewer-provided information and any assessment content as confidential. Live help that misrepresents who is answering (for example, undisclosed real-time AI feeding answers) can breach the employer's rules or disqualify a candidate. **Prepare with practice, not substitution. Check each employer's stated policy on tools.** — Advice (no source)

### E. Format, culture and inclusion
14. Greenhouse's 2025 AI in Hiring report says 54% of US job seekers surveyed had encountered an AI-led interview [17][opened]. — Medium (vendor survey)
15. UK government material says candidates can request adjustments such as additional time in a recruitment process, and that the duty to adjust applies when the employer knows or could reasonably be expected to know about a disability [18][19][snippet]. Other countries differ: say "check locally". — Medium
16. "I" versus "we" (finding 6) comes from US career services. Whether a modest "we" is preferred in other markets is a convention I did not source; ask the candidate. — Unverified
17. "Overqualified" can act as an age proxy. The advice to answer the underlying concern is practitioner guidance, not sourced. — Unverified
