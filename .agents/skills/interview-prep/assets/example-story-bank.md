# Alex Morgan - Story Bank

### Cutting merchant onboarding from 9 days to 2
**Competencies:** initiative, problem-solving, influence
**S:** At Payly, new merchants waited an average of nine days to start taking payments, and activation had stalled. Support was spending most of its time chasing stuck applications.
**T:** I owned the onboarding funnel and had two quarters to improve activation without raising fraud losses.
**A:** I interviewed 30 merchants and mapped where applications stalled. Most waited on manual identity checks. I persuaded risk and compliance to pilot automated checks with a third-party provider, wrote the success criteria with them, and ran the pilot on 10% of traffic before scaling. I then rebuilt the status emails so merchants always knew what was happening next.
**R:** Onboarding fell from nine days to two, onboarding support tickets dropped 38%, and fraud losses stayed flat. I learned to agree the risk metric with compliance before proposing the change, not after.

### Disagreeing with engineering on a launch date
**Competencies:** conflict, teamwork, pressure
**S:** Two weeks before a pricing launch, my engineering lead told me the data migration was not safe to ship on the planned date. Finance had already built its forecast around that date.
**T:** I had to protect the commitment to finance without pushing the team into a risky release.
**A:** I asked her to walk me through the three risks she was worried about. Two could be mitigated with a feature flag and one needed an extra week of testing. I proposed a staged release: new customers on the planned date, existing customers a week later. I took the revised plan to finance with the revenue impact quantified at £120K for the one-week delay.
**R:** We shipped on time for new customers, migrated existing accounts with no incidents in the first 2 weeks, and finance accepted the plan. We now hold a joint risk review one month before every launch.

### A pricing experiment that failed
**Competencies:** failure, learning, problem-solving
**S:** I led an experiment to move small merchants to usage-based pricing, expecting higher revenue per account.
**T:** I was accountable for the hypothesis and for the readout to leadership.
**A:** After four weeks, churn among the smallest merchants had risen 6 points, well above our agreed threshold. I stopped the test early and analysed churn by segment. The lowest-volume merchants were most price-sensitive. I presented the result honestly, including my wrong assumption, and proposed a tiered model with a free usage band.
**R:** The tiered model later raised average revenue per account by 12% with no increase in churn. I now test price changes on one segment at a time and set the stop-loss before the test starts.

### Leading a squad through a reorganisation
**Competencies:** leadership, ambiguity, pressure
**S:** A reorganisation moved my team of 14 under a new director and froze two roadmap commitments. Two engineers had started interviewing elsewhere.
**T:** I needed to keep delivery on track and keep the team together while the structure was unclear.
**A:** I held a team session that same week to share what I knew and what I did not. I agreed three priorities with the new director, wrote them down, and cancelled four lower-value projects. I met each engineer one-to-one to hear their concerns and gave two of them ownership of the new priorities.
**R:** We delivered all three priorities within the quarter, no one on the team left that year, and the engagement score rose from 68 to 79. I learned to share what I do not know early, because silence was what worried people most.

### Turning around an unhappy reseller partner
**Competencies:** customer, influence, initiative
**S:** Our largest reseller partner, a software vendor that offers Payly to its own small-business customers and accounts for about 9% of our payment volume, threatened to leave after repeated reporting outages.
**T:** I was asked to lead the recovery plan alongside account management.
**A:** I joined their weekly call for six weeks and asked their finance lead which reports mattered most to their small-business customers. I got engineering to prioritise those three reports over planned feature work and explained the trade-off to our own leadership. I sent a fortnightly status note, gave them a named contact for escalations, and set up automated alerting so we found issues before they did.
**R:** Report availability rose from 94% to 99.8%, the partner renewed for two years, and they became a reference account. I learned that a partner in trouble mostly wants a single accountable person and honest updates.

### Learning a new domain quickly
**Competencies:** learning, ambiguity, initiative
**S:** I moved from invoicing software into payments with no background in card schemes or regulation, and my first project involved 3DS authentication rules.
**T:** I had to be credible with risk and legal within a few weeks.
**A:** I asked a risk analyst to teach me for an hour a week, read the scheme rulebooks for the three flows we used, and wrote a one-page explainer for the team that she reviewed. I paired with an engineer to trace one real transaction end to end.
**R:** Within six weeks I was leading design reviews on 3DS flows, and the explainer is now part of onboarding for new product managers. I learned that asking one expert for a regular hour beats reading alone.
