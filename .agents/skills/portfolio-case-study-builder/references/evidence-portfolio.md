# Evidence: Portfolios and Case Studies

Use in **drafts** only (`[^n]` plus a definition line from the Bibliography). Do not cite in the published case study.

## Rules and honest limits
- **The evidence base is thin.** Almost all of it is guidance from design-education sites, bootcamps and vendor blogs, mostly about UX and product design. I found no controlled study of what makes a case study win interviews.
- Applying it to other fields (engineering, data, writing, marketing, consulting) is **by analogy**. Say so and adapt section names to the field.
- Sources tagged `[snippet]` were read from search results only.
- Confidentiality and permission advice has no source here. It is basic professional practice, and it is the user's responsibility. Never imply legal certainty.

## Findings

### A. What hiring teams look for (commonly advised)
1. Hiring teams skim for signals: how you framed the problem, what you prioritised, how you handled constraints, and whether the outcomes make sense [1][snippet]. — Low
2. A strong case study tells a clear story: the problem, your role, research, key insights, decisions and why, testing and iteration, the outcome, and what you learned [2][snippet]. — Low
3. Hiring managers want to see how you think, not just what you produced, so focus on problem-solving, evidence and rationale [3][snippet]. — Low
4. Make the process visible: discovery -> decision -> outcome [4][snippet]. — Low (product management portfolios)

### B. How many and how long
5. Three to five strong, well-documented case studies are advised over many half-finished projects [5][snippet]. — Low
6. Another source suggests two to three case studies [2][snippet]. — Low. **The sources disagree; choose by the target role and the quality of the material.**
7. Set the scene briefly: two to three short sentences on what the product was, who used it and why it mattered [6][snippet]. — Low

### C. Accessibility
8. Accessible documents use real headings in order, descriptive link text and alt text for images [7][8]. — Medium (university accessibility guidance)

### D. Confidentiality (no source)
9. Only publish work you have permission to share. Remove client names, internal tools, people, URLs and real data where required, or rebuild examples with synthetic data. — Practice, not law. The author decides and is responsible.
