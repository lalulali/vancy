# Evidence: Salary and Offer Negotiation

Use in **drafts** only (cite as `[^n]`, definition line copied from the Bibliography). Never cite in an email or message sent to an employer.

## Rules and limits
- Sources tagged `[snippet]` were read from search results only.
- **Most anchoring research comes from lab experiments and general bargaining**, not salary talks specifically. Job-offer negotiation is multi-issue and relational, and employers often have fixed bands. Say "research suggests", never "this will get you X%".
- The skill does not know market pay. **Never state a salary figure as a market fact unless the user supplied it or you cite a source you opened.**
- No legal or financial advice. Pay-transparency and salary-history rules vary by place and change, and I verified only the examples cited below. Anywhere else, tell the user to check locally.
- Statistics that I could not verify from the original paper are labelled unverified; do not quote them.

## Findings

### A. First offers and anchoring
1. Across three experiments, whichever party made the first offer obtained a better outcome, and first offers strongly predicted final settlement [1][snippet]. — Medium-High (peer-reviewed lab studies)
2. Harvard's Program on Negotiation summarises that making the first offer can anchor a negotiation in your favour, citing Galinsky & Mussweiler [2][snippet]. — Medium
3. PON reports that, per a 2009 study by Galinsky, Ku and Mussweiler, the first offer explains between 50% and 85% of the variation in final outcomes. The same PON article summarises a separate study (Loschelder et al.) in which first movers did better in multi-issue talks when they did not reveal their priorities in the opening offer [3][opened]. — Medium. (Reported secondhand from the papers; the 2009 study is not specific to multi-issue negotiations.)
4. A meta-analysis found ambitious first offers beat moderate ones on agreement value, but it also reports a risk of impasse. The title refers to both the "power and peril" of first offers [4][snippet]. An effect size (g = 1.14) appeared in a search snippet and **I could not verify it, so do not quote it.** — Medium (direction only). **Implication: open high enough to matter and low enough to defend with evidence.**
5. Columbia's information-anchoring model says first offers help or hurt depending on context: they pull outcomes toward the anchor but also reveal information [5][snippet]. — Medium. **Implication: when you know little about the employer's budget, listen first or ask for the range.**

### B. Practical process (career services)
6. Negotiating is the discussion of an offer; employer and candidate are on the same side. Consider the whole package: salary, benefits, relocation, advancement and development [6]. — Medium (university career services)
7. Ask for time to consider an offer and agree a timeline rather than accepting on the spot [7]. — Medium
8. Ask for the total compensation package in writing: salary, commission, bonuses, paid time off and benefits [8][snippet]. — Medium
9. A counteroffer is typically polite and concise, thanks the employer, states the request, and gives supporting evidence or justification. The employer then accepts, declines or negotiates [9][snippet]. — Low-Medium (career-advice site)
10. Salary is one part of total compensation; also weigh time off, health and development [10][snippet]. — Medium

### C. Pay transparency and past-pay questions
11. **US:** rules are set by state and city and differ in what they cover. Examples from government pages (their servers refused my fetch, so I read only the search snippets; re-check each page before relying on it): Colorado requires employers to disclose pay (or a range) and benefits in job postings [14][snippet]; California bars employers from seeking an applicant's salary history [15][snippet]; New York bars wage-history inquiries, but an applicant may volunteer it without prompting [16][snippet]. Payroll-vendor roundups say salary-history bans exist in 20+ states [11][12][snippet]; I did not verify the count, so tell the user to check the state or city page. — Medium
12. **EU:** Directive (EU) 2023/970 gives job applicants the right to the initial pay or range, in the vacancy notice or before the interview, and bars employers from asking about pay history [17][snippet]. The transposition deadline (7 June 2026) has passed. As at 12 October 2026 only Italy, Lithuania, Malta and Slovakia had met it. Greece's law takes effect on 1 November 2026, Estonia has partly implemented, and others (for example Spain, Denmark, France, the Netherlands, the Czech Republic, Finland, Bulgaria) have drafts or bills in progress [18][opened]. Timing differs by country (a draft Danish bill requires disclosure before the interview, not in the ad; a Czech draft would require it before the contract is concluded) [18]. A law-firm briefing adds that the Directive does not oblige employers to put the range in the advert, but an applicant may ask for it at any stage of recruitment, and employers may not ask about pay history [19][opened]. Both sources are law-firm updates; the national statute is what binds, so the user must check it. — Medium (law-firm updates)
13. Lithuania is reported to require pay ranges in vacancy ads [13][snippet]; it is also on Deloitte's list of countries that transposed on time [18]. — Low-Medium (aggregator)
14. **UK, Canada, Australia, Singapore, India, the Gulf and other places:** not researched here. Say "check locally".
**Implication: before advising on what to say about current pay or about asking for ranges, ask the user where the job is. Where the employer is in an EU country, tell them to look up the national statute that implements the Directive; where it applies, they can ask for the range before interview and need not answer pay-history questions. Elsewhere, tell them to check current local rules.**

### D. Claims to avoid
15. Stating that "x% of people who negotiate get a raise" or a fixed average gain without a source. I found no reliable figure for this. — Do not state.
16. Telling a user a specific dollar value they "should" ask for without their sources. — Do not do this.
17. Treating equity as cash at face value. Public-company RSUs are liquid once vested (a modest haircut of roughly 10–25% for price risk is a common working assumption, not a sourced figure); private-company equity is illiquid and may be worth nothing (70–100% haircut is a cautious working assumption, also unsourced); options are worth the spread over the strike, not the grant value. Tax at vesting or exercise differs by country: check locally. — Working assumptions, unsourced
