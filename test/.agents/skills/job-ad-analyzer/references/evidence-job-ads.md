# Evidence: Reading Job Ads and Deciding Whether to Apply

Use in **drafts** only (cite as `[^n]`, where n is a **Bibliography** number, with a definition line copied from the Bibliography). Never put citations in a final cover letter or CV. Cite only sources listed here.

## Rules
- Sources tagged `[snippet]` were read from search results only. Use their direction, not their exact numbers.
- Most of this is career-advice and survey material, not controlled research. State it as guidance ("commonly advised"), not as law.
- Numbering: findings are labelled E1, E2 and so on. A plain `[n]` after a finding is a Bibliography entry, never a finding. Cite the Bibliography entry, not the finding label.
- Evidence supports a reading strategy. It never supports a claim about the candidate.

## Findings

### A. How strictly to read "requirements"
E1. SHRM advises candidates to ask first whether they can perform the **core functions** of the job and can make a credible case. A required licence, security clearance or legally mandated credential can be a true barrier. A specific platform, years of experience or a "preferred" skill is often more flexible, depending on employer and role [1]. — Medium (professional-body advice)
E2. SEEK suggests that meeting around 70% of the criteria, **especially those near the top of the list**, may still be worth applying for [2][snippet]. — Low-Medium (advice, no method given)
E3. A Forbes piece citing an Indeed survey says employers frequently consider applicants who meet about 70% of the qualifications [3][snippet]. — Low (secondary report of a survey; underlying figure not checked)
E4. Indeed's job-seeker research found that a sizable number of people opt out of applying when they might actually be good candidates [4][snippet]. — Medium

### B. A statistic to avoid
E5. The "men apply at 60% of the qualifications; women only at 100%" claim is widely repeated and attributed to an internal Hewlett-Packard report. I could not open the original report. It appears only in secondary sources [5][snippet]. — **UNVERIFIED. Do not cite it as fact, and do not use it to tell any user what "people like them" do.**

### C. Why wording matters downstream
E6. Recruiters mostly use an applicant tracking system (ATS) to search and rank. 76.4% in Jobscan's analysis search by skills taken from the job description [6]. So the ad's own vocabulary is the vocabulary a recruiter searches for. — Medium (vendor analysis)
E7. Over 90% of employers in the HBS/Accenture "Hidden Workers" study (2021) use systems to filter or rank middle- and high-skill candidates, so rigid criteria can screen out qualified people [7][snippet]. — High
E8. In a 25-recruiter US interview study, knockout questions (work authorisation, licences, location, education) were the one automatic filter recruiters described: 84% rely on them, and all used them where configured. Content-based auto-reject was configured by 2 of 25 [8]. — Medium (small sample). **Implication: treat work authorisation, licences, and location as hard filters even when the rest of the ad is flexible.**
E9. Copying wording from the posting is risky: 32% of hiring managers in CareerBuilder's 2013 survey named it as a mistake that may cause dismissal [9]. — Medium (dated, US-only). **Mirror the vocabulary, do not paste sentences.**

### D. Fairness and legality of ad content
E10. Age, marital status, religion and similar attributes are protected in many jurisdictions. In the US, the EEOC lists the prohibited practices, including discriminatory job advertisements [10][snippet]. The EEOC page's search snippet lists race, colour, religion, sex, national origin, age (40 or older), disability and genetic information as protected under the laws it enforces. — Medium. **If an ad asks for protected attributes, flag it for the user without giving legal advice.**

### E. Ghost jobs, scams and discriminatory wording
E11. Greenhouse's 2025 AI in Hiring report (surveys in the US, UK, Ireland and Germany) says 69% of the US job seekers surveyed had encountered fake job postings [11]. I opened the press release (2025-11-19) to confirm the figure. It measures what candidates say they encountered, not verified fakes, and the survey was run by an ATS vendor. — Medium (vendor survey)
E12. The US FTC lists job-scam signs. The 2023 consumer alert [12] (opened) names a recruiter emailing from a personal address such as gmail or yahoo rather than a company address, pressure to pay (a fake invoice for equipment or "training"), and requests for a driver's licence, Social Security or bank account number before any job details. The FTC's Job scams page [13] (opened) names paying for the promise of a job, starter kits, training or certifications, placement fees, and the fake-cheque pattern (deposit a cheque, send part of it on, and the cheque later bounces). Treat the same signs as red flags in any country. Neither page mentions WhatsApp or Telegram-only contact; that sign is common in reported scams but is not sourced here. — Medium-High (regulator consumer advice)
E13. Ghost-job signals (a posting open or reposted for 30–45+ days, "talent pipeline" or "continuous recruiting" wording, no named team or manager, not on the company's own careers page, recent layoffs or a hiring freeze) come from practitioner advice. **No source was found for them and no prevalence statistic was verified (practitioner advice; unverified), so use them as prompts to ask, never as proof. Do not cite a bibliography entry for them.** — Unverified
E14. In the US the EEOC says the ADEA generally makes age preferences in job notices or advertisements unlawful, and it lists discriminatory job ads among prohibited practices [10][14][snippet]. Rules differ by country and state, so flag age-coded or non-job-related wording without giving legal advice. — Medium
